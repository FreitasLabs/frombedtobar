The Rosemary Is Always There

Some herbs come in waves. Basil has its summer rush. Mint spills over when the weather warms. Lavender has its bloom window. Rosemary is different.

It stays. Through heat, cool mornings, holiday meals, quick weeknight dinners, and slow drinks on the patio, rosemary is the herb we keep reaching for.

It is often the first thing clipped for the kitchen and the last thing still giving when the rest of the garden quiets down.

Rooted in the garden. Raised around the table.

The garden-to-glass path

Rosemary is not just a garnish. It is a foundation.

A strong rosemary plant connects the herb bed to the kitchen, the bar, and the table. It can flavor a syrup, smoke a cocktail, finish roasted potatoes, perfume bread, or turn sparkling water into something that feels complete.

What rosemary wants

  • Full sun: At least six hours of direct light.
  • Fast drainage: Raised beds, gravelly soil, or containers work well.
  • Air movement: Avoid cramped, soggy corners.
  • Light watering: Water deeply while establishing, then let the plant dry down.

What usually goes wrong

Wet feet
Root rot is the main reason rosemary fails. If the soil stays damp, move it to a raised bed or container.
Too much shade
Rosemary will survive in some shade, but it will grow softer, weaker, and less flavorful.

How to Harvest Rosemary Without Hurting the Plant

The best rosemary harvests are small and regular. You are not cutting the plant back hard. You are taking flexible green tips and shaping the plant as you go.

01

Cut green tips

Harvest young, flexible stems rather than old woody branches.

02

Never remove too much

Take no more than one-third of the plant at a time.

03

Prune after flowering

A light trim after spring flowers keeps rosemary compact and productive.

04

Avoid cutting into old wood

Rosemary often will not regrow from bare gray stems.

Questions from the herb bed

Rosemary FAQ

Rosemary is forgiving once it is established, but it has a few firm rules: sun, drainage, restraint with water, and a light hand when harvesting.

Yes, once established rosemary is one of the most self-sufficient plants in the garden. It tolerates drought, poor soil, and minimal attention. The main requirements are full sun and good drainage — get those right and rosemary will grow into a substantial, long-lived shrub that keeps giving for years. The main failure point is wet, cold soil in winter, which causes root rot.

Any upright-growing Rosmarinus officinalis variety works well for cocktails — the flavour difference between cultivars is subtle compared to, say, lavender varieties. Tuscan Blue is a classic choice with strong fragrance and upright growth that makes harvesting easy. Arp is more cold-hardy and a better choice for gardens in zones 6 and below. Avoid prostrate or trailing varieties for cocktail use — they produce shorter stems that are harder to harvest and use as garnishes.

Rosemary grows very well in containers, particularly in wetter climates where pot drainage is easier to control than in-ground drainage. Use a terracotta or unglazed ceramic pot for breathability, a free-draining mix with added grit or perlite, and position in full sun. Bring containers indoors or into a sheltered spot in zones below 6 where hard frosts are regular.

Rosemary is a perennial evergreen shrub in zones 7 through 11 — it keeps its leaves year-round and grows continuously without dying back. In zones 6 and below it can be grown as a perennial with protection, or treated as an annual. Unlike mint, rosemary doesn't die back in winter in milder climates, meaning you can harvest small amounts year-round even when the garden is otherwise dormant.

Combine equal parts sugar and water in a saucepan over medium heat. Once the sugar has dissolved, remove from heat, add 3 to 4 fresh rosemary sprigs (or 2 dried), and steep for 20 to 30 minutes. Taste frequently — rosemary is assertive and over-steeping produces a medicinal, bitter result. Strain, cool, and refrigerate in a sealed jar for up to 3 weeks. Bourbon and rye are the most natural partners — the piney, resinous character of rosemary has an affinity with the caramel and oak of aged American whiskey. Gin works well, particularly with styles that already have herbal botanical profiles. Mezcal is a compelling match where rosemary's smokiness and the spirit's smokiness amplify each other. Tequila blanco with rosemary and grapefruit is a very good combination. Rosemary is less successful with light rum or vodka, where it can read as medicinal rather than herbal.

Yes — dried rosemary works well in syrups and infusions, and dried stems make excellent garnishes that hold their structure beautifully. For syrups, use roughly half the quantity of dried versus fresh. For garnishes, a dried stem with its needles intact laid across a glass is one of the most striking simple presentations from the bar garden. Fresh rosemary is preferable for muddling, but for everything else dried is a legitimate and often more convenient choice.

Briefly pass a dried rosemary sprig through a flame, blow it out, and place it across the top of the finished drink. The smoldering needle tips release a thin plume of piney, herbaceous smoke as the drink is served and first approached — the nose experiences the smoke before the palate experiences the drink. It works particularly well on bourbon and mezcal cocktails. Keep the burn brief; a heavily charred sprig smells unpleasant and the smoke becomes acrid.

The most common cause is root rot from wet soil — rosemary simply cannot survive in poorly drained conditions, especially over winter when the combination of cold and moisture is lethal. Other causes include planting in too much shade (rosemary needs at least 6 hours of sun), or cutting back into old wood during pruning, which the plant cannot regenerate from. Rosemary is assertive and resinous — enough to overpower a drink if used carelessly. In a syrup, 3 to 4 sprigs per cup of liquid is the right starting point, steeped off the heat for 20 to 30 minutes with frequent tasting. As a muddled ingredient, one or two young tips gently pressed is enough — crushing hard or muddling a whole sprig releases harsh tannins from the woody part of the stem. As a garnish, one good stem is more than sufficient.
